## Fact notes

- **$34 vs $39, "about a third more":** Eric Anderson and Duncan Simester's women's clothing
  catalog experiment: a dress sold about a third more at $39 than at $34 (and $44 did no better
  than $34). Anderson and Simester, "Mind Your Pricing Cues", Harvard Business Review, September
  2003; summary at https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/yours-only-1999 and
  https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-big-difference-a-penny-makes
- **"Round signals quality", "many restaurant managers":** in a survey of restaurant managers
  (Schindler, Parsa and Naipaul, Cornell Hospitality Quarterly), 42.9% believed guests link
  prices ending in .00 with high quality. Phrased as managers' practice, not a proven guest
  effect. Supporting lab work: Wadhwa and Zhang, "This Number Just Feels Right", Journal of
  Consumer Research 41(5), 2015 (round prices fit feeling-driven purchases; a 2018 replication
  was mixed, so it isn't quoted on screen).
- ".95 says bargain": the common reading of just-below prices as a discount cue (Anderson and
  Simester, above).
- The fix is a judgment call drawn from the above, not a statistic.

## Post caption

$9.95 and $10 are a nickel apart, but they don't say the same thing. Guests read the first digit, so .95 says deal. A round number says this is worth it. Use .95 on grab-and-go items, round off your signature dishes, and keep one style per section of the menu.
