# markup-vs-margin

**Topic:** Markup and margin are not the same thing
**Kind:** advice (money)
**Hook type:** hook-same ("Markup" ≠ "Margin")
**Background:** paper (pad: warm)
**Voice:** speech, Roma Tom narrates
**Length:** about 32 s

The idea: owners often add a 50% markup and think they keep half of the sale. Markup is
measured on the cost, margin on the price, so a 50% markup only keeps a third. To hit a margin,
price from the margin.

## Fact notes

All money figures are **example numbers**, framed in the voice with "Say a croissant costs…".

- **Cost to make, $3.00:** example all-in cost of one croissant (ingredients, packaging, a share
  of labor).
- **Menu price, $4.50:** 50% markup on cost: 3.00 × 1.5 = **$4.50**.
- **You keep, $1.50:** 4.50 − 3.00 = **$1.50**.
- **Markup 50%:** profit ÷ cost = 1.50 ÷ 3.00 = **50%** (the standard definition of markup).
- **Margin 33%:** profit ÷ price = 1.50 ÷ 4.50 = **33.3%**, "a third" (the standard definition of
  gross margin).
- **Price from margin:** price = cost ÷ (1 − margin). For a 50% margin: 3.00 ÷ (1 − 0.5) =
  3.00 ÷ 0.5 = **$6.00**. Check: (6 − 3) ÷ 6 = 50%.

## Post caption

Markup and margin sound like the same thing. They're not. Say a croissant costs 3 dollars to make and you add 50 percent: it sells for 4.50 and you keep 1.50. That's a 50 percent markup on the cost, but only a 33 percent margin on the price. Want to keep half of every sale? Divide the cost by one minus the margin: 3 dollars divided by 0.5 is 6 dollars.
