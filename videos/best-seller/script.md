# best-seller

**Question:** "My burger outsells everything. Why doesn't the money feel like it?"
**Background:** paper
**Length:** 22.4 s

The idea: the dish you sell most isn't always the one that earns most per plate. Cost every dish,
then let the menu nudge guests towards the quiet earners.

## Fact notes

All figures are **example costs for a typical independent cafe**, not data from a real business.
They are there to show the method; the post caption says so.

- **Burger, $16:** beef patty and bun $3.90, cheese and pickles $1.10, fries $0.90, sauce $0.30.
  Ingredient cost $6.20 (38.8% of the price). Left per plate: 16.00 − 6.20 = **$9.80**.
- **Mushroom pasta, $14:** pasta $0.60, mushrooms $1.40, cream and parmesan $0.90, herbs and oil
  $0.20. Ingredient cost $3.10 (22.1%). Left per plate: 14.00 − 3.10 = **$10.90**.
- **Difference per plate:** 10.90 − 9.80 = **$1.10**.
- **The jar:** 1.10 × 20 plates a week × 52 weeks = **$1,144** a year.
- "Left per plate" counts ingredients only (gross profit per plate). Labour, rent and card fees
  come out of it too, but they're about the same for both dishes, so they don't change the comparison.
- The fix steps are general menu-engineering practice: cost each dish, give high-margin dishes
  the best spots on the menu, and test small price rises on the dishes people order anyway.
